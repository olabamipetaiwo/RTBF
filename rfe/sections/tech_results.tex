\section{Post-Deletion Behavior}
    \label{sec:tech_results}

    Deleting did not reliably keep a fact out of later conversations, and what happened depended more on the agent than on the deletion method or how the request was worded. The agent's reply and the interface did not show a user which outcome they had. This section reports what happened after deletion (Sections~\ref{sec:overall} to \ref{sec:what-agents-say}) and where it differs from what participants expected (Section~\ref{sec:expectation}).

    \begin{takeawaybox}
    \textbf{Finding:} The outcome depended on the agent. Where we could judge, the fact stayed out of later conversations on Claude and Copilot and was often recall success on ChatGPT and Perplexity, and how the request was worded did not explain the difference. Agents mostly replied that they had complied or partly complied, in about seven in ten of the deletion requests typed in the chat, so the reply did not show the outcome.
    \end{takeawaybox}
    
    \subsection{Overall Behavior of Agents}
    \label{sec:overall}

    We could judge fewer than half of the test cases, and among those about four in ten were recall success. Of the 865 test cases, 394 (45.5\%) were recall success or recall failed, and 162 of them (41.1\%) were recall success. The other 471 (54.5\%) were can't tell, because we cannot show that the fact reached the place we tested (Section~\ref{sec:didr}). So at least 18.7\% of all deletions failed, and the true share could be higher.

    No data deletion method held in every test case we could judge. The four methods we tested through UI actions were recall success in 29\% to 50\% of their test cases, but with 7 to 13 test cases each, we do not rank them. Sending a deletion-request prompt, the method we tested most, was recall success in 144 of 811 test cases and can't tell in 454. So for most deletions through prompts the outcome is can't tell, and we cannot say whether the fact is gone.

    The agent mattered more than the method (Table~\ref{tab:outcomes-agent}). ChatGPT was mostly recall success and Claude mostly recall failed, and the other four agents were mostly can't tell (56\% to 100\% of test cases, by agent). So the pooled share hides that nearly three quarters of all recall success come from ChatGPT.

    Uploaded files form a separate group of 12 test cases, of which we ran 11 (the most extensive deletion on Perplexity needs a paid plan). After deleting the conversation or applying the most extensive deletion, the fact was not retrieved in a new conversation in any of them (recall failed).

    \subsection{Cross-Agent Difference}
    \label{sec:cross-agent}

    The agents differed in what happened after a deletion request, and the differences are consistent with how each agent is built and what its documentation says, not with how the request was worded. We take the wording first, because it is the explanation we can rule out.

    What an agent did with a deletion request typed in the chat falls into three kinds. Claude and Copilot deleted: where we could judge, the test cases were recall failed, and the agent had shown it saved the fact. ChatGPT and Perplexity did not: the test cases were recall success in most of ChatGPT's and a minority of Perplexity's. Both also sometimes withheld the fact, saying we had asked them to delete or not use it, and for Perplexity this was most of the test cases that were can't tell. For Gemini and DeepSeek every such test case was can't tell.

    Among the 350 deletion requests typed in the chat with a known outcome, the wording of a request did not explain the differences. For each of the seven coded dimensions, such as where the request asks to delete, how it is phrased, and whether it gives a reason, we compared the outcome across its codes. Recall success never occurred under any code on Claude or Copilot, and on ChatGPT it occurred in half or more of the test cases under every code with at least six requests. Perplexity varied more across codes, but the codes that differed held few requests. We leave out Gemini and DeepSeek, because every one of their test cases is can't tell. So which agent received a request mattered more than how it was worded.

    The agents mostly did what their documentation says, and that did not mean the fact was gone. We take the documentation as the behavior each provider says to expect. Among the deletion requests typed in the chat, 319 test cases had a verdict and 1 contradicted the documentation. In 107 of the 318 consistent test cases the fact was recall success, all on ChatGPT, whose documentation says that deleting a saved memory does not remove mentions of that information from past conversations. For Gemini and Perplexity there is no commitment to compare the result with, and DeepSeek's documentation does not address a deletion request. Through UI actions the pattern was the same: 22 of the 23 test cases with a verdict matched the documentation, and in 6 of the 22 the fact was recall success.

    The remaining differences are consistent with where each agent keeps a fact. Claude's help page says that memory entries made from a deleted conversation are not removed, and deleting a conversation was recall success in all 3 UI-action test cases, while deleting a saved memory was recall failed in all 3. On ChatGPT, the memory page no longer showed the fact in 112 of the 118 test cases where it was recall success, so we cannot attribute those to saved memory. DeepSeek has no memory page to read, which is why every one of its test cases is can't tell. So a deletion method that acts on one place did not remove a fact kept in another.

    \subsection{What Agents Say}
    \label{sec:what-agents-say}

    Replies that complied or were partial were far more common than deletions shown to hold: 70.9\% of the 798 deletion requests typed in the chat got such a reply, while the deletion was shown to hold in 26.4\% and could not be judged in 56.1\% (Table~\ref{tab:replies-outcome}). A reply did not tell the user what happened. Among the test cases with a known outcome, the same kind of reply came with recall success in none of Claude's and Copilot's and in most of ChatGPT's and Perplexity's, whether it complied or was partial. After a deflected reply, most test cases were can't tell (86\%). So the same kind of reply could come with a deletion that held, one that failed, or one we could not judge, depending on the agent.

    Some replies showed that the agent remembered the deletion request. In a new conversation, ChatGPT and Perplexity sometimes withheld the fact, saying that we had asked them to delete it or not use it, as in ``I can't provide that name because you previously asked me to delete the information about your letterpress kit.'' Among the test cases that were not recall success, this happened in 52\% to 95\% of ChatGPT's and Perplexity's, and in none of the other four agents'. So for Perplexity, most test cases that are can't tell show the agent still held the earlier request and said it would not use the fact.

    Only Gemini answered some requests with an app-permission card. It showed one for 6.0\% of its deletion requests typed in the chat, mostly for a fact about a project or plan, which Gemini may have read as a note or message to delete in a Google app. Nothing was deleted, because we declined every card. No other agent answered the same prompts this way, so the reply came from the agent, though the content of the request may have prompted it. All of these test cases are can't tell.

    \subsection{User Expectation vs. Behavior}
    \label{sec:expectation}

    \begin{takeawaybox}
    \textbf{Finding:} Users and agents differed in two ways. The fact stayed retrievable in a new conversation after a deletion that participants expected to keep it out, and the signals participants used to verify a deletion did not show the outcome.
    \end{takeawaybox}
    
    Users and agents differed in two ways. The fact stayed retrievable in a new conversation after a deletion that participants expected to keep it out, and the signals participants used to verify a deletion did not show the outcome.

    Participants expected a deletion to keep the information out of later conversations, and our test speaks to that expectation directly. For the four most chosen data deletion methods, 42.5\% to 63.4\% of responses expected the agent not to bring the information up in future conversations (Section~\ref{sec:qual_results}). Recall success contradicts this expectation and recall failed is consistent with it. The other expectation, permanent deletion from the database and backups (52.1\% to 65.9\%), we cannot observe, so recall success contradicts it but recall failed does not confirm it.

    Table~\ref{tab:expectation-outcome} sets each method's expectation beside its outcomes. For every method, the fact was recall success in some test cases, so the expectation was not met even for methods that about half of the responses expected to keep it out. For deletion-request prompts, 454 of the 811 test cases are can't tell, so the expectation was tested in 357. The survey counts responses and the test counts test cases, so we compare the pattern and not the percentages.

    The first signal was the agent's reply to the request, and it said a deletion was done far more often than we could show it held. Participants wanted to be told what a deletion did (41.2\% wanted a breakdown of what was deleted and what may be retained), yet 70.9\% of the replies to the deletion requests typed in the chat complied or were partial, while the deletion was shown to hold in 26.4\% of those requests and could not be judged in 56.1\% (Table~\ref{tab:replies-outcome}).

    The second signal was asking the agent again in the same conversation, and it often disagreed with the outcome. Participants who deleted by sending a deletion-request prompt mostly verified this way (53.8\% of those responses). Among the test cases with a known outcome, the agent's reply when asked again disagreed with the outcome in 26\% to 78\%, depending on the agent. On ChatGPT the agent mostly did not repeat the fact although the outcome was recall success, and on Claude it repeated the fact although the outcome was recall failed, so a user who asked this way would conclude that the deletion held on ChatGPT when it had failed, and that it failed on Claude when it had held. Gemini and DeepSeek are left out, because every one of their test cases is can't tell.

    The third signal, the interface, was no better a guide. For deletions through UI actions, the interface changed in cases such as a conversation missing from the history list or an entry missing from a memory page, and among those with a known outcome 42\% were recall success. A user who expects the information to stay out of later conversations therefore had no signal that confirmed it, and 44.6\% of responses said they did not know how to verify a deletion at all.

    So expectation and behavior differ where an agent kept the fact after a deletion that participants expected to keep it out of later conversations, and that depended on the agent (Section~\ref{sec:cross-agent}). The documentation did not close the gap: for the deletion requests typed in the chat, the documentation of Gemini, Perplexity and DeepSeek made no commitment that applied, and for ChatGPT it says that mentions in past conversations remain after a saved memory is deleted. Neither the agent's reply nor the interface showed which case a user was in.
