\begin{abstract}
% Draft, placed 2026-10-28 (task item 24). The TBWL inside is the main-result sentence from item 11; the platform pattern depends on Copilot's recall finishing.
Commercial chatbots keep what users tell them in conversations and saved memories, and users can delete it in several ways, but it is unclear what each deletion removes or whether users can tell. We combine a survey of 177 chatbot users with an audit of six chatbots (ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Claude, Perplexity and DeepSeek). The survey asks what users expect deletion to do and how they check that it worked. The audit shares a fact with each chatbot, deletes it, and asks for it again in a new conversation about a month later, then compares what comes back with what the platform documents and what users expect. \textbf{\{TBWL\}}. Participants most often expected deletion to be permanent (59.0\% of 354 observations), and 44.6\% of the observations reported not knowing how to verify that it worked. In the audit, whether the fact came back depended mostly on the platform and hardly on how the request was worded. A chatbot's reply and its screen did not show whether the fact was gone, and asking again in the same chat misled users. Three of the six platforms document little about what deletion does. Gemini sometimes answered a deletion request by asking permission to turn on an app and deleted nothing (6.0\% of 133 participants' requests). We recommend that platforms state what each control removes, say what a deletion did and did not do, and give users something they can check.
\end{abstract}

Commercial chatbots routinely receive personal and sensitive information, and under Article 17 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) individuals may, under certain conditions, request erasure of personal data concerning them~\cite{gdpr2016}.
In a chatbot, a fact can persist in several places: the conversation history, saved memories (facts a chatbot keeps about a user across conversations), and other platform data stores, and models can also reproduce memorized text~\cite{carlini2021extracting}.
Platforms offer several ways to delete, but what each one removes is often unclear.

Prior work has documented inconsistencies in the privacy controls of commercial chatbots~\cite{li2026privacy}, but it has focused on the controls available, not on what happens after a user invokes them.
Studies of search, cloud storage and website privacy controls have found gaps between what users expect and what systems do when data is deleted~\cite{bertram2019five,kang2015mydata,ramokapane2017stupid,habib2019empirical}.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\xw{decouple background and related work}

Article 17 of the GDPR gives people, under specified conditions, a right to have personal data erased~\cite{gdpr2016}, and the California Consumer Privacy Act gives California residents a right to request deletion of personal information a business has collected~\cite{ccpa2018}. Commercial LLM chatbots do not offer one such action. A single account may expose conversation deletion, memory deletion, typed deletion requests, and account-level controls, each with a different scope.

Chatbot users have five main ways to delete: they can ask the chatbot to forget something in a message, delete one conversation, clear all conversation history, delete one saved memory, or clear all saved memories. Some platforms add controls of their own, such as ChatGPT's custom instructions or Copilot's privacy dashboard, and users can also delete the account or file a request with the company. Which of these exist depends on the platform (Table~\ref{tab:methods}). The list covers what a user can do from a consumer account; it is not a complete account of what a company can do internally.

We report three things for each test. The \textit{acknowledgment} is what the chatbot says in reply to a deletion request. The \textit{recall outcome} says whether the deleted information came back 31 days later: \textit{recall success} (it came back), \textit{recall failed} (it did not), or \textit{inconclusive} (we cannot tell, for example because we cannot show that the chatbot ever stored it). \textit{Policy consistency} says whether the outcome matches what the platform's documentation promises. Section~\ref{sec:audit-methodology} defines each precisely.

In a chatbot, a fact a user shares can sit in several places, and a deletion control may reach only some of them. The conversation history keeps what was said in that chat. Saved memories are facts the chatbot keeps about the user across conversations, and some platforms let users review and delete them. Some platforms also keep custom instructions, and a platform may use conversations to train its models, which deleting a record does not undo~\cite{bourtoule2021machine}. A control that clears one place leaves the others, so what a deletion removes depends on where the fact was kept.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Deletion rights are hard to exercise and check even outside chatbots. Bertram et al.\ analyze five years of Google's right-to-be-forgotten requests and show the scale and complexity of administering erasure~\cite{bertram2019five}, and Habib et al.\ find that website deletion and opt-out controls can be difficult to locate, poorly explained, or ineffective~\cite{habib2019empirical}, and Liu et al.\ show that mobile-app account deletion is hard to find and use, and that users' expectations differ from how apps are designed~\cite{liu2022zombie}.

Audits of deletion exist for online services, but not for chatbots. Rupp et al.\ requested erasure from 90 online services and, more than six months later, used GDPR access requests to see what data remained; they found 27\% of services non-compliant, with results that differed between a dedicated deletion button and a formal erasure request~\cite{rupp2022leave}.

Users hold incomplete models of deletion. Kang et al.\ document incomplete mental models of online data handling~\cite{kang2015mydata}, and Ramokapane et al.\ show that cloud users struggle to tell where deleted data may persist~\cite{ramokapane2017stupid}. For chatbots, Zhang et al.\ find that users of memory features hold incomplete models of what is stored and want finer control over reviewing, editing, and deleting memories~\cite{zhang2025understanding}, and Chen et al.\ report that interviewees doubted deletion works and asked how they could get ``proof'' that information had been removed~\cite{chen2026relational}, and Kwesi et al.\ find that, among privacy controls for emotional disclosures to chatbots, deletion controls most increased participants' willingness to use a chatbot, yet more than a third of their 354 participants doubted that controls would function as promised~\cite{kwesi2026impact}.

Work on LLM platforms describes the controls but does not test their outcomes. Li et al.\ examine the access, edit, delete, and sharing controls of six conversational platforms and find substantial variation~\cite{li2026privacy}; our audit tests what happens after those controls are used on live accounts and compares the outcomes with documented commitments. Machine-unlearning work shows that removing a stored record does not remove its influence on a trained model~\cite{bourtoule2021machine}. Our audit does not verify model-level unlearning, so a user-facing control may establish less than it appears to.

To our knowledge, no work tests what happens after a chatbot user deletes. We connect these threads by combining a black-box audit of six commercial chatbots with a survey of deletion choices, expectations, and verification. Like Rupp et al., we check after a delay, but through what a user can observe and not through access requests to the company, so that documented commitments, observed behavior, and user expectations can be compared within one study.

The behavioral audit shows what platforms do when a user deletes data. To read those results against what users expect, we surveyed people who have deleted data from a chatbot about how they delete, what they expect deletion to do, and whether they can tell that it worked.

The survey had seven kinds of questions (Appendix~\ref{sec:instrument}). Screening and consent checked age, U.S. residence, and prior chatbot use. Background questions covered which chatbots participants use, for how long, and which deletion methods they had used. Participants chose among eight deletion methods: asking the chatbot to forget in a message, deleting a single conversation, clearing all conversation history, deleting a specific saved memory, clearing all saved memories, deleting the account, using a privacy dashboard, and filing a request. In each of two scenarios, one less sensitive and one more sensitive, participants chose a deletion method and marked which methods would be ideal, rated the chosen method on perceived protection, effort, and benefit loss, then reported their willingness to delete, what they expected deletion to do, and how, if at all, they had checked. A deletion-experience section asked what data participants had tried to delete, why, how they learned about deletion options, whether they had seen deleted information referenced again, and what confirmation they receive and want. A privacy-knowledge quiz tested understanding of privacy policies, the right to be forgotten, training data, and memory. A final set asked about attitudes toward chatbots and sharing data, social-network views, and self-rated ability to work with AI, followed by demographics.

We ran the survey online in Qualtrics and recruited participants through Prolific, an online platform for research participants. We set no formal power-based sample-size target and aimed to recruit approximately 200 participants. The design was within-subject, so each participant saw both scenarios, one less sensitive (LS) and one more sensitive (MS), in random order. Both used the same questions, with the three scales containing 5, 4, and 3 items.

Participants completed two chatbot deletion scenarios. In the less-sensitive scenario (\textbf{LS}), the user mentioned a city while asking about the weather; in the more-sensitive scenario (\textbf{MS}), the user mentioned a home address while seeking a clinic for treatment of a sexually transmitted infection. After each scenario, participants were asked to imagine deleting the shared location data. Analyses combining both scenarios are labeled \textbf{Pooled}. Two instructed-response items assessed attention. The full scenarios, checks, and scale items appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:instrument}.

After exclusions, 177 participants remained. Of 235 Qualtrics submissions, we excluded 17 incomplete responses, one failed attention check, and 36 respondents who had never deleted chatbot data, leaving 181 eligible participants; four did not complete both scenario method-choice items. Scenario order was randomised (83 participants saw LS first, 94 saw MS first) and did not change the results (Appendix~\ref{sec:stats-details}).

Unless stated otherwise, scenario-level percentages use these 177 participants. Analyses pooling both scenarios treat each participant-scenario response as one observation ($n=354$) while accounting for repeated measures by participant. Expectation, verification, and pooled factor analyses use this observation-level sample. Method-specific pooled analyses are restricted to methods meeting the minimum group-size criterion described below. Eligibility was restricted to respondents who had previously deleted chatbot data. Accordingly, all survey analyses describe users with prior experience using at least one deletion control and do not represent respondents who had never attempted chatbot-data deletion.

Because we ran many tests on a modest sample, the analyses are exploratory. We adjust $p$ values for the number of tests (Holm correction, reported as $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}$; unadjusted values are $p$), and read a nonsignificant result as finding no association, not as proof that none exists. Each participant answered twice, so analyses that combine both scenarios use models that account for this (generalized estimating equations clustered by participant). We compare only the four deletion methods chosen by at least 14 participants, because smaller groups are too small to compare. To check that the three rating scales (protection, effort, benefit loss) measure separate things, we used confirmatory factor analysis (Appendix~\ref{sec:stats-details}); tests and correction families are in Table~\ref{tab:analysis-families}.

\subsection{Deletion-Request Corpus}
\label{sec:prompt-corpus}

One arm of the behavioral audit (\S\ref{sec:audit-methodology}) uses participant-written deletion requests rather than researcher-written ones. After selecting a deletion method in each survey scenario, participants wrote the deletion request they would send to the chatbot. Responses failing the attention check were excluded.

Across both scenarios, 209 participants submitted 415 nonblank deletion requests. After removing within-participant repeats and cross-participant duplicates, 375 distinct deletion requests remained. Unlike the main survey analysis, the corpus was not restricted to participants with prior deletion experience because the request-writing task was hypothetical and concerned phrasing rather than prior behavior. Residual identifying information was redacted from 7 requests, all involving place names.

Auditing all 375 requests on six platforms would have required 2,250 request-platform pairs under free-tier rate limits, so we audited a sample. Before any codebook existed, we grouped every request with keyword rules into seven coarse groups (for example, whether it mentioned memory, the conversation, the account, or a database), solely to spread the sample across kinds of request. We then drew 138 requests by stratified random sampling over these groups, allocating in proportion to group size with a minimum of 15 per group, so that the two smallest groups were included in full. The keyword groups are not used in any analysis. Each selected request was used in the behavioral audit (\S\ref{sec:audit-methodology}), reproduced verbatim except for substitution of the topic.

Two researchers independently coded the 138 sampled requests. They first coded a subset of 50 without a shared codebook, reconciled the candidate dimensions into a common codebook, and refined it, including the deletion-location definition, through further independent rounds on that subset. Both coders then independently coded all 138 requests on seven dimensions: deletion location, justification, data type, grammatical mood, tone, verb register, and accompanying secondary request. Table~\ref{tab:ac1} reports agreement for this final round. Disagreements were then resolved jointly through discussion, and the resolved codes are used in all analyses. Under these codes, the sample spans all six deletion-location categories, from 6 requests that asked to delete the whole account to 56 that named no location.

Inter-rater reliability is measured with Gwet's $AC_1$~\cite{gwet2008ac1}, which is less sensitive than Cohen's $\kappa$ to the prevalence problem affecting dimensions dominated by a single category~\cite{gwet2008ac1,wongpakaran2013comparison}.

\subsection{Behavioral Audit}
\label{sec:audit-methodology}

\xw{a few students ask the question of "how do you check whether LLM agents actually deleted your data"? The goal of the behavioral audit is to see what a user can observe after asking a chatbot to delete something: does the information still come back, and does that match what the platform says it does? It is a user-side test, not a technical analysis of how the chatbots store data or how their models work, so it cannot show whether data was removed from backend storage.

We audited six chatbots: ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, Claude, Perplexity, and DeepSeek. They are the major general-purpose consumer chatbots available in the United States, the market our survey participants come from. Our survey screened for prior use of at least one of them, and each was used by at least a third of participants (from 36.2\% for DeepSeek to 99.4\% for ChatGPT, of 177). They also differ in ways that matter for deletion: they keep a disclosed fact in different places (the conversation, a separate memory, account activity), offer different deletion controls, and come from different developers, including one outside the United States. The six therefore cover a spread of consumer chatbot designs, and our results describe these designs, not every chatbot.

We conducted a black-box behavioral audit of the consumer-facing deletion controls of these chatbots. Tests were run through each platform's live web interface rather than its API because the relevant memory-feature, settings, and deletion controls are exposed only through the consumer interface.

Below we define a test case, explain how we kept test cases apart, describe when and how we checked recall, and explain how we scored the outcomes.

We chose the injection and deletion methods to match how people use and delete chatbot data. Before the audit, we walked through each platform's web interface and listed every way a user can put information in and take it out, an approach similar to earlier walkthrough work on LLM privacy controls~\cite{li2026privacy}. Information can enter in three conversational ways: mentioned in passing (I1), given as an explicit instruction to remember (I2), or entered on a memory or settings page (I3). Each reaches a different place where a platform may keep it, which is why we test them separately. The deletion methods are the controls users actually have, and they match the methods our survey participants know (asking the chatbot to forget, deleting one conversation, clearing all history, deleting one saved memory, clearing all saved memories). For each platform we also run one maximal combination of all its controls, the most a careful user could do, to see whether anything still comes back. We left out settings that only switch a feature off, since they stop future collection and delete nothing already stored. We also left out account deletion, since once the account is gone there is nothing left to test, and requests filed with the company, which are handled outside the chatbot where we cannot observe the result.

Uploaded files are different from messages: a platform can read a document within a conversation, save it to memory, do both, or do neither. We therefore tested uploads as a separate group of 12 test cases, two deletion methods on each platform, using one fixed document, a short personal notes page with the token among filler lines, and we checked separately whether the file was read and whether it was saved.

A \textit{test case} combines one injection method, one deletion method, and one platform. In each test case, we tell the chatbot something personal; we call this the \textit{disclosure}. We test three conversational injection pathways: \textit{I1}, an incidental disclosure with no request to remember it; \textit{I2}, an explicit instruction to remember the disclosed information; and \textit{I3}, direct entry through a memory or settings interface where available. 

% A fourth pathway, \textit{FILE}, shares the information through an uploaded PDF. Each disclosure is about an everyday \textit{topic} (for example, ``a new houseplant'') and includes a \textit{token}, a made-up code name that is unique to that test case. If the token comes back later, we know which test case it came from. We wrote one topic per test case by hand and built each token by randomly drawing two to four words from a public word list, skipping sexual, violent, drug-related, and offensive words.

Deletion methods follow the controls each platform exposes, including conversation deletion, individual or bulk memory deletion, and account- or privacy-level deletion. Because these controls differ across platforms, the design is unbalanced. The final audit contains 85 test cases: 73 conversational injection--deletion combinations and 12 file-upload test cases.

For RQ2, each audit test case is assigned a \textit{commitment type} based solely on the platform's published documentation for the deletion action and injection surface. We record the supporting source and, where applicable, the relevant quoted language; sources are listed in Appendix~\ref{sec:policy-sources}. \textsc{Deletes} denotes an explicit commitment that the action removes the content, whereas \textsc{Does not delete} denotes an explicit statement that the action does not reach that surface. \textsc{No commitment} indicates that the documentation is silent, \textsc{Unrelated} that the deletion method does not apply to the injection surface, and \textsc{Not testable} that the surface was inaccessible on the test account.

Only \textsc{Deletes} and \textsc{Does not delete} support policy-consistency verdicts; all other test cases are reported descriptively. Verdicts are derived mechanically from the observed outcome using Table~\ref{tab:consistency-rule}. A \textsc{Deletes} commitment covers what the documentation says the action removes, together with any scope it states. Where it says that past conversations remain, a recall success solely from those conversations is consistent with it.

The participant-written requests sampled in \S\ref{sec:prompt-corpus} form a separate extension to the audit of 85 test cases. Each request is issued to all six platforms after a fixed I1 disclosure, with only the disclosed topic substituted, so injection is held constant and deletion wording is the varying factor. Recall uses the same R1/R2/R3 recall checks as the 85 main test cases.

Five of the 138 sampled requests were excluded because they requested deletion of all user or chat data and could therefore affect other test cases sharing the same platform account. The remaining 133 refer to a single disclosed item, yielding 798 request-platform pairs. Each platform uses a dedicated account separate from those used for the 85 main test cases, with unique topics and tokens used to attribute recall to individual test cases.


Because accounts are reused across test cases, each test case is assigned a unique topic and a hard-to-guess token, following canary-style memorization tests~\cite{carlini2019secret}. Recall of a token therefore identifies the test case from which it originated. Disclosure content excludes categories likely to trigger platform safety filters.

Token-level attribution is insufficient for account-wide deletion actions that may affect multiple test cases. Such test cases are therefore isolated on dedicated accounts when they could interfere with another test case or its 31-day recall window. For participant-written deletion requests, test cases share one dedicated account per platform, but five requests asking for deletion of all user or chat data are excluded because they could delete other test cases' content. As an additional safeguard, deletion actions sharing an account are scheduled from narrowest to broadest, ensuring that blanket deletion actions run only after test cases with a narrower scope have completed.

Each test case is executed in three scheduled stages: injection, deletion 48 hours later, and recall 31 days after deletion. The 48-hour interval allows time for asynchronous processing of saved memories before deletion, while the 31-day recall window accommodates platforms that document deletion completion within 30 days and provides a common interval across all six systems. Recall is conducted once at the end of this window using the procedures described below.
\xw{are these probes indeed implemented?}

At each recall point, three recall checks are run in new conversations. \textit{R1} (direct recall) asks two differently worded open questions about the disclosed topic. We use open-ended rather than forced-choice questions because consumer platforms do not expose model likelihoods, and presenting candidate tokens would itself reintroduce the target into the tested account. \textbf{R2 (settings recall) inspects the platform's memory feature page, where available.} \textit{R3} (indirect recall) issues two tasks about the topic that do not ask for its name and do not contain the token, testing whether the disclosure surfaces in ordinary output without a retrieval request.

We use three recall checks because each can see something the others cannot. R1 asks directly, as a user trying to find out whether something was kept would. But a chatbot can hold information and still not volunteer it. R3 therefore never asks about the topic and checks whether the disclosure shows up in ordinary tasks, which is how stored information would affect a user in practice. R2 does not rely on the chatbot's answer at all: it reads the memory page the platform shows to users, so it can confirm that an entry is still stored when the chatbot says nothing. R2 can only be used where a platform has such a page, so it does not apply to DeepSeek, Perplexity (whose memory is not available on the free tier we tested) or Copilot.

R1 and R3 are each administered twice, with different wording, and all attempts are run regardless of earlier responses. Responses are automatically matched against the test case's unique token, with ambiguous matches routed for human review. When Gemini responded by asking permission to turn on one of its apps, the request was declined and the check scored as a non-answer. R2 is manually coded according to whether the disclosed information appears in the platform's memory feature page.

The checks run in the fixed order R2, R3, then R1. This minimizes contamination between the checks: R2 is observational, R3 precedes any direct retrieval request, and R1 runs last because it explicitly names the topic.

For deletion requests issued within the original chat, we additionally repeat the first R1 question in that conversation after completing the new-conversation recall checks. This measures whether the platform continues to use the disclosure within the conversation where the deletion request was made. Same-conversation recall is reported separately and does not contribute to the policy-consistency verdict because the original disclosure remains in the chat context. It is not applicable when the deletion removes the conversation or is performed through settings, interface controls, or maximal combinations.

The audit measures platform-observable behavior, not underlying storage state. Each test case records three outcomes: the platform's acknowledgment, the recall outcome at the 31-day recall point, and consistency with the platform's documented commitment. A \textit{recall success} means the fact came back, which is a bad sign for deletion; \textit{recall failed} means it did not. The recall outcome is assessed through the R2 settings inspection, R3 indirect check, and R1 direct-recall check.

These observations cannot establish whether data were removed from backend storage, training data, or model parameters. A recall failed outcome means only that the token did not appear through the tested surfaces, while a recall success shows that the disclosure remained accessible through at least one of them. Accordingly, RQ1 and RQ2 concern what users can observe and policy consistency, not verified backend deletion.

\textit{Acknowledgment} records what the platform says in response to the deletion request.

The recall outcome is determined from the new-conversation recall checks. A test case is a \textit{recall success} if any recall check brings back the token, \textit{recall failed} only when none does and the injection is known to have reached the layer being tested, and \textit{inconclusive} otherwise. This prevents absence from being treated automatically as evidence of deletion.

Policy consistency is derived from the documented commitment together with the observed interface and recall outcomes using the fixed rules in Table~\ref{tab:consistency-rule}. Conversational acknowledgment is reported separately and does not itself determine whether a documented policy commitment was satisfied.

A recall failed outcome is interpreted as evidence of successful removal only when there is evidence that the platform retained the disclosure before deletion. Rather than running an additional undeleted control for every test case, we infer pre-deletion retention from the platform's reply and interface at injection.

Retention is coded from platform-visible signals. Only explicit interface indications that the fact was saved or replies explicitly stating that it was stored are treated as evidence of retention; ambiguous acknowledgments or absent signals are not. Accordingly, test cases without such evidence are classified as inconclusive when recall later fails.

\textbf{Audit analysis.} For comparisons across platforms and methods, we reduce each test case to one question: did the fact come back in a new conversation? Recall success counts as yes, recall failed and inconclusive count as no, and test cases never recalled are left out. We report this as how often the fact is retrieved, not as a deletion rate, because on some platforms a no can also mean the fact was never stored.

We run three analyses, after checking that their assumptions hold (independent test cases). First, each of the 138 deletion requests was run on every platform, and we report each platform's retrieval rate with a Wilson 95\% interval. Second, in the main grid, we compare test cases where the documentation says the method deletes the stored fact with those where it says it does not or does not apply, using Fisher's exact test. Third, we ask whether the way a request is worded (its coded dimensions) relates to retrieval, using counts with Wilson intervals only and no tests, since few test cases fall in each group. As with the survey, these analyses are exploratory.




Acknowledgment is recorded from the platform's single response to the deletion request because repeating the request could alter the state being measured. For chat-based deletions, acknowledgment is recovered from the post-deletion screenshot.

Recall uses a fixed repetition policy across all test cases: R1 (\textit{direct recall}) and R3 (\textit{indirect recall}) are each administered twice because chat-based responses are non-deterministic and a second attempt reduces the risk of a false negative. R2 (\textit{settings recall}) is inspected once because it is a deterministic interface check: the token is either visible on the memory-feature or settings page or it is not. Both R1 and R3 attempts are reported separately.

Each phase of a test case, including injection, deletion, new-conversation recall, and same-conversation recall where applicable, is preserved with a transcript and browser screenshot for independent review. All accounts use each platform's free tier. The audit records tier-gated surfaces as inaccessible rather than omitting them.


Two platforms changed during data collection. Copilot migrated to a new web interface between injection and recall. Claude also replaced its memory feature, clearing unrecalled memories independently of our deletion actions. We therefore re-injected the 17 affected Claude test cases with fresh tokens and restarted their deletion and recall timelines from that point.

\subsection{What Participants Do and What They Would Prefer}
\label{sec:sensitivity-effect}
Users want to delete their data, more so when it is sensitive, but they rely on the few controls they found on their own, and the method they would prefer, asking the chatbot to forget, is one they rarely use. Participants were more willing to delete their data in MS than in LS ($M=4.37$ vs.\ $3.98$; $\Delta=+0.39$, $p<.001$, $r=.55$), so greater perceived privacy risk strengthens the motivation to use deletion controls. Their experience, however, was concentrated in interface-based methods, particularly deleting individual conversations and clearing conversation history (Table~\ref{tab:method-usage}); memory-specific actions, typed deletion requests, and formal privacy channels were less common. Most participants found these controls on their own, through platform settings, experimentation, or external documentation, and direct guidance from the platform was uncommon. In both scenarios, deleting a single conversation was the most common choice, followed by clearing all conversation history, and method choice did not differ significantly between scenarios.

The method participants ranked as ideal was different. Deleting a single conversation was again the most preferred in both scenarios, followed by asking the chatbot to forget (Table~\ref{tab:method-usage}), and preferences did not differ significantly across scenarios. Yet asking the chatbot to forget was much less prominent in past use and in actual scenario choices, a gap between the deletion experience users would prefer and the methods they currently use. So the question is whether the controls people actually use do what they expect, which we turn to next.

\subsection{Evaluation of Data Deletion Methods}

Participants believed that deletion works, whichever method they used, but most had no way to check that it did. The three survey scales were validated beforehand (\S\ref{sec:survey-methodology}), so they can be compared across methods.

Only the four methods chosen by at least 14 participants (single conversation, clear all history, specific memory, ask to forget) enter the method comparisons below; the others are too rare to compare. Scale scores are the mean of a scale's items on a five-point agreement scale. Pooled figures use participant-scenario observations ($n=317$ across the four methods), so a participant who chose the same method in both scenarios contributes two.

\subsubsection{Methods Differ Only in Effort}
\label{sec:effort}
Participants rated the four methods as equally protective of their privacy and equally costly in lost personalisation; they told the methods apart only by effort. Neither protection nor benefit loss differed significantly across methods (Table~\ref{tab:method-scales}; benefit-loss regression, all $p\geq.10$). Effort was the only scale on which methods differed significantly: single-conversation deletion was rated lowest and specific-memory deletion highest; in pooled contrasts, both single-conversation and ask-to-forget were significantly less effortful than specific-memory deletion, and single-conversation was less effortful than clear-all-history. Method choice was self-selected, so these are associations, not effects of switching methods. Methods operating on finer-grained objects (a specific memory) are perceived as more effortful than those operating on coarser units (a conversation), while asking the chatbot to forget is perceived as comparably easy to the simplest interface action. So the methods look the same to participants except in effort.

\subsubsection{Participants Expect Permanent Deletion but Cannot Check It}
\label{sec:expectations}



Across methods, participants most often expected permanent deletion (59.0\% of 354 observations), suggesting that they expect deletion to mean the data are gone rather than merely hidden, and expected the chatbot not to refer to the data in future conversations (54.2\%); training-data exclusion was a minority expectation (27.7\%). Participants choosing specific-memory deletion were also more likely to expect the chatbot to stop referencing the data in the current conversation, consistent with a mental model linking saved memories to ongoing conversational behaviour. Expectations did not differ significantly across scenarios or deletion methods (Table~\ref{tab:contingency}), although some method-level differences were sizable and should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of equivalence.

Yet participants had little means of checking. The most common response was not knowing how to verify that deletion had worked (44.6\% of 354 observations), and those choosing single-conversation deletion were especially likely to report no way of checking (Table~\ref{tab:contingency}). Those choosing ask-to-forget most often said they had checked by asking again in the same conversation. Verification strategies did not differ across scenarios but did vary by deletion method: conversational deletion offers users an intuitive verification channel, while interface-based deletion provides less obvious feedback about whether the requested deletion has taken effect.

Some participants had already been let down: 23.7\% of 177 reported noticing a chatbot reference information they believed they had deleted, while 49.2\% had not and 24.3\% were unsure. They also wanted clear confirmation. Only 41.2\% of 177 currently receive a clear message confirming deletion (34.5\% a vague acknowledgement, 29.4\% none), whereas 68.4\% want a popup confirmation and 41.2\% want a breakdown of what was deleted and what may be retained. The demand for a breakdown shows that users want transparency not just about whether data were removed, but about what may remain, a concern directly relevant to the partial-deletion outcomes the behavioral audit examines. These are the expectations the audit tests.

\subsection{What Chatbots Said and Did}

When asked to delete, chatbots replied in very different ways, and what they said did not predict what happened.

\subsection{Platforms Differ and Wording Hardly Matters}
\label{sec:audit-platforms}
Whether the fact came back depended on the platform: the same 138 deletion requests, sent to every platform, brought it back on only two (Table~\ref{tab:retrieval}).

A zero here means the fact did not come back, not that it was deleted, because on some platforms the fact may never have been stored.

ChatGPT and Perplexity did not bring back the same requests: a request that came back on one platform was no more likely to come back on the other (Fisher's exact $p=.80$).

How a request was worded changed the outcome on only one platform.
On ChatGPT, the fact came back in half or more of the requests at every level of every wording feature we coded, with no consistent pattern.

Gemini and DeepSeek are left out of this comparison, because every one of their cases where the fact did not come back is a case where we cannot tell whether the deletion worked or the fact was never stored, so wording has nothing to explain there.

\subsection{What the Results Mean}
\label{sec:discussion-results}
\textbf{\{TBWL\}}

We read the results in four steps: where expectations and behavior diverged, why users could not see it, what the documentation offered, and what the audit cannot check.

Users' expectations of deletion held on some platforms and failed on others.
Participants most often expected deletion to be permanent (59.0\% of 354 observations) and expected the chatbot not to mention the fact in future chats (54.2\%) (\S\ref{sec:expectations}).
The audit found the fact came back on some platforms and not on others, and the outcome varied by method as well (Table~\ref{tab:gap-method}), so the same expectation met different results depending on where and how a user deleted.

Users could not see the gap, because the checks available to them can mislead.
Prior work on cloud-storage and website deletion shows that offering deletion does not ensure users can locate or verify it~\cite{ramokapane2017stupid,habib2019empirical}.
Our survey shows the same for chatbots: 44.6\% of 354 observations reported not knowing how to verify that deletion had worked (\S\ref{sec:expectations}).
The audit adds that the checks users do have were unreliable: a platform's reply and the screen did not show whether the fact was gone, and asking again in the same chat told ChatGPT users the fact was gone when it came back in a new conversation.

Documentation could not settle the question for half the platforms.
ChatGPT, Claude and Copilot document that their methods delete, and their behavior almost always matched.
Gemini, Perplexity and DeepSeek document little about the method, so a user of these cannot learn from the documentation what deletion does, and the gap is one of information as well as behavior.

The audit cannot check one expectation, training-data exclusion, which a minority of participants selected (27.7\%).
Removing stored records does not necessarily remove their influence on a model already trained on them, which machine unlearning treats as a separate problem~\cite{bourtoule2021machine}.
The question was select-all and did not separate preventing future training use from removing influence already learned, so we read the result as an expectation pattern, not a misconception, and as a scope limit of the audit, not a mismatch.

Two survey findings frame what platforms could do about this.
Deleting one saved memory was rated more effortful than the other methods (\S\ref{sec:effort}), which fits prior work showing users hold incomplete mental models of what chatbot memories contain~\cite{zhang2025understanding}, and participants were more willing to delete in the more sensitive scenario, where deletion controls already differ across platforms~\cite{li2026privacy}.
So the gap lies in what users can see and what platforms say about deletion, which is where our recommendations aim.

\subsection{What We Recommend}
\label{sec:discussion-recommend}
The results point to three changes platforms could make, each aimed at what users can see and what platforms say about deletion.

Platforms should state what each deletion control removes and what may remain.
Participants most often expected deletion to be permanent, yet the outcome depended on the platform and the method, and three of the six platforms document little about the method we tested.
A control's description should name what it covers, such as one conversation, saved memories or the whole account, and say plainly what it does not touch.

A platform's reply to a deletion request should say what it did, not only that it is done.
Replies ranged from confirming the deletion to sending the user elsewhere, and on ChatGPT a reply saying the deletion was done did not mean the fact stayed gone.
A reply should state the action taken and what remains, and a request the chatbot cannot act on should be declined as such, not answered by offering an unrelated app, as Gemini sometimes did.

Platforms should give users something they can check.
44.6\% of 354 observations reported not knowing how to verify that deletion had worked, and the checks users had, such as the screen or asking again in the same chat, could show the fact as gone when it was not.
A visible record of what is saved, with an observable confirmation of the outcome, would let a user check without relying on the chatbot's own answer.

These recommendations rest on what six chatbots did when tested from the user's side and on self-reported survey answers, not on an intervention, so whether such changes improve users' understanding or behavior remains to be tested.
So the aim is that what a user is told, shown and able to check matches what deletion did; whether deletion removes the data from backend storage is a different question, which only a technical audit can answer.

\subsection{What a Technical Audit Would Add}
\label{sec:discussion-technical-audit}
We tested from the user's side because it is the vantage point open to anyone outside a platform, and it is the one users actually have.
Our behavioral audit shows what a user is told, shown and can find after asking to delete, and whether that matches what the platform documents.
It needs no cooperation from the platform and can be repeated on any consumer chatbot, and it found what a look at stored data would not, such as replies and screens that said the fact was gone when it was not.

A technical audit, which would inspect what a platform stores, needs access that platforms do not currently give.
It would have to examine backend storage, logs and backups, and for training data it would have to evaluate a trained model, which is a separate problem from deleting records~\cite{bourtoule2021machine}.
That access is realistic only through a cooperating provider or a regulator with the power to inspect, not from outside, so we see such an audit as future work that builds on ours and not as a replacement for it.

Deleting a fact from a chatbot is a hard technical problem, so a deletion that reaches the screen can leave the fact in other places.
A fact a user shares can be copied into stored conversations, saved memories, logs, backups and derived data, and a deletion request removes it only where the platform has built a path to do so.
Work on secure deletion shows the same difficulty in ordinary storage systems: whether removed data is really gone depends on the layer of the system the deletion reaches~\cite{reardon2013sok}.
For the model itself, removing records does not remove what a trained model has learned from them~\cite{bourtoule2021machine}, and models can reproduce memorized training text~\cite{carlini2021extracting}.
This difficulty is a plausible reason why platforms differ in what their controls reach, and why a reply or screen that says a fact is gone is weak evidence that it is.
Our recall checks see only what a user can see, so a technical audit should expect most of these places to be out of its view too.

So the two kinds of audit answer different questions: ours asks what users can observe and rely on, and a technical audit would ask what is actually stored.
Our results suggest which controls, which requests and which platform behaviors such an audit should examine first.

\section{Conclusion}

We studied what happens when a chatbot user asks to delete, from two sides: what users expect and how they check, and what six chatbots do after a deletion request. \textbf{\{TBWL\}} Participants expected deletion to be permanent, but whether a fact came back depended on the platform and the method. The checks users rely on, such as a chatbot's reply, the screen, or asking again in the same chat, did not show whether the fact was gone, and for half the platforms the documentation said too little to tell users what deletion does.

Our audit reports what a user can observe, not what a platform stores. A technical audit of stored data is the next step, and it will need access that platforms do not yet give, because deletion may itself be technically hard.

\section{Statistical Details}

\textbf{Scenario order.} The two scenarios were presented in random order (83 participants saw LS first and 94 saw MS first; binomial $p=.45$). Presentation order did not moderate the within-participant differences between LS and MS in willingness, protection, effort, or benefit loss (Mann-Whitney $U$, all $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=1.00$), nor was it associated with whether participants changed deletion method between scenarios (Fisher's exact $p=.35$).

\textbf{Pooled models.} For analyses pooling both scenarios, we fit generalized estimating equations (GEEs) clustered by participant, with deletion method and scenario as predictors, an independence working correlation, and robust standard errors. Rating outcomes use an identity link, while binary expectation and verification outcomes use a logit link. Method effects are assessed with omnibus Wald tests, followed by pairwise post hoc contrasts only when the Holm-adjusted omnibus test is significant. \section{Survey Instrument}
\label{sec:instrument}

\textbf{Scenarios.} Each scenario opened with the text ``Imagine that you
are chatting with an AI chatbot\ldots'' followed by the conversation
below and the instruction to delete the data shared in it.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textit{Less sensitive (weather).} User: ``I am in Orlando now.
What's the weather today in Orlando?'' AI: ``Based on your city, it's
72\,$^{\circ}$F and sunny. There is a slight chance of rain this
evening.'' User: ``Should I bring an umbrella?'' AI: ``Probably not. The
chance of rain is only 10\%.'' \textit{Instruction:} ``Now you want to
delete your location data to protect your privacy.''
\item \textit{More sensitive (health).} User: ``I live at 7482 Orange
Blossom Lane, Orlando, FL 32801. Can you find me the nearest Medical
Center to get treatment for sexually transmitted infections?'' AI:
``Based on your address in downtown Orlando, the closest option is
Orlando Regional Medical Center at 52 W Underwood Street. If it's a
life-threatening emergency, call 911 rather than driving yourself.''
User: ``How should I prepare?'' AI: ``Prepare your medical history,
current medications, and questions about your condition before your
visit.'' \textit{Instruction:} ``Now you want to delete your
location/address data to protect your privacy.''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scales.} In each scenario, respondents rated the deletion method
they chose on a five-point agreement scale (Strongly Disagree,
Disagree, Neutral, Agree, Strongly Agree). The three scales, with their
items, are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textit{Perceived protection} (5 items): ``This data deletion method
contributes to the protection of my privacy''; ``\ldots helps me to
protect my personal information''; ``\ldots effectively reduces the risk
of my data being misused''; ``\ldots gives me complete control over my
personal data''; ``By using this data deletion method, I can decide what
personal data the AI Chatbot retains about me.''
\item \textit{Effort} (4 items): ``This data deletion method takes too
much time''; ``\ldots is overly complex to use''; ``I find it difficult to
navigate this data deletion method''; ``Understanding how this data
deletion method works requires significant effort.''
\item \textit{Benefit loss} (3 items): ``Using this data deletion method
would prevent AI Chatbots from providing responses tailored to my
activity context''; ``\ldots would reduce the relevance of assistance
tailored to my preferences''; ``Using this data deletion feature would
prevent AI Chatbots from providing the kind of help I need.''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Attention checks.} ``Please select `Sometimes' for this question''
(frequency scale from Never to Always) and ``Please select `Agree' for
this question'' (agreement scale).
