\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Hundreds of millions of people now share personal, and often sensitive,
information with commercial large language model (LLM) chatbots as part
of daily use. The right to erasure, enshrined in Article 17 of the
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), entitles individuals to
request that personal data concerning them be deleted under certain
conditions \cite{gdpr2016}. Commercial LLM chatbots such as ChatGPT,
Gemini, Claude, Copilot, Perplexity, and DeepSeek retain user data in
several, distinct forms: raw conversation history, chatbot-extracted
``memories'' or saved facts that persist across sessions, and, per each
platform's own privacy policy, conversation content that may be used to
train future models, a risk made concrete by work showing that LLMs can
memorize and later regurgitate verbatim fragments of their training
data~\cite{carlini2021extracting}. Each retention surface exposes its own,
differently labeled deletion control: deleting a single conversation,
clearing all conversation history, deleting one saved memory, or asking
the chatbot in natural language to ``forget'' something. No
evidence establishes whether invoking any of these controls satisfies an
RTBF-style erasure request across every form in which that data persists.

Closest to this setting, Li et al.\ audit the stated privacy controls of
six conversational LLM platforms and find them inconsistently
implemented; most do not support deleting an individual
message~\cite{li2026privacy}. That walkthrough characterizes what each
platform's interface exposes, but does not test whether invoking a
control on a live account removes the data it names, nor survey users
about what those controls are believed to accomplish; \S\ref{sec:background}
situates it fully relative to this paper's contribution.

Whether the same expectation-behavior divergence documented for other
deletion mechanisms (search delisting at
scale~\cite{bertram2019five}, and cloud-storage and website opt-out
controls~\cite{kang2015mydata,ramokapane2017stupid,habib2019empirical})
holds for LLM chatbots' several, differently labeled deletion controls is
largely untested; \S\ref{sec:background} reviews that literature in full.

This ambiguity sits on both sides of the interaction. On the system
side, it is unclear whether a given deletion control removes data from
the surface it names, and whether that removal extends to derived
retention such as training data. On the user side, it is unclear
whether people select deletion methods that match what they believe
those methods accomplish, and whether they ever verify that outcome. A
mismatch on either side undermines a de facto right to erasure: for
users who cannot control data they do not know persists, or who choose
a lighter-weight action while believing it accomplishes more than it
does.

This study addresses the following research questions, split by system and
user side, plus their divergence:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item[] \textbf{RQ1} (system, behavior): How do commercial LLM chatbots
    actually handle data deletion requests?
  \item[] \textbf{RQ2} (system, policy gap): Where does observed deletion
    behavior contradict each platform's stated policy?
  \item[] \textbf{RQ3} (user, expectation \& verification): What do users
    believe deletion accomplishes, and do they verify that it worked?
  \item[] \textbf{RQ4} (user, sensitivity): Does data sensitivity change
    deletion method choice, willingness to delete, or expected effort/loss?
  \item[] \textbf{RQ5} (divergence): Where does user expectation diverge
    most from audited platform behavior, and who bears that gap?
\end{itemize}

This paper reports two paired components addressing all five: a
participatory survey (RQ3, RQ4) and a technical audit of deletion
controls across the same six commercial platforms (RQ1, RQ2, RQ5).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\textbf{Right to erasure, at scale.} The GDPR's Article 17 right to
erasure~\cite{gdpr2016} has mostly been studied through its search-engine
delisting mechanism: Bertram et al.\ analyze five years and 3.2 million
URLs submitted to Google for right-to-be-forgotten delisting, finding
requests concentrated on a small number of sites and requesters, and
documenting the operational difficulty of adjudicating erasure at
scale~\cite{bertram2019five}. That line of work audits a single,
well-defined deletion mechanism (search delisting) end to end. Commercial
LLM chatbots instead expose several, informally specified deletion
controls over the same underlying account (conversation-level deletion,
memory-level deletion, natural-language ``forget'' requests, and
account-level deletion), which is the gap this paper's technical audit
(RQ1/RQ2/RQ5) targets.

\textbf{Auditing LLM platform controls.} Closest to this paper, Li et
al.\ walk through the stated access/edit/delete/share
controls of six conversational LLM platforms (a partially overlapping
set with the six audited here; see \S\ref{sec:intro}) and find them inconsistently
implemented, with most platforms not supporting deletion of an individual
message~\cite{li2026privacy}. That audit characterizes what controls each
platform's interface exposes; it stops short of testing, on live
accounts, whether invoking a given control actually removes the data it
names (the system-side gap RQ1/RQ2 target), and it does not survey users
about those controls. This paper's technical audit and participatory
survey close these two gaps respectively.

\textbf{Machine unlearning.} A deletion request that only removes a
record from a database does not, by itself, remove that record's
influence on a model already trained on it. Bourtoule et al.\ formalize
this distinction and propose \textit{SISA} training to make exact
unlearning practical, framing verifiable removal of a data point's
influence as a distinct technical problem from simply deleting the
underlying record~\cite{bourtoule2021machine}.

\textbf{User mental models of deletion.} Usable-privacy research has
repeatedly found that people's mental models of ``deletion'' do not
match how deletion is actually implemented. Kang et al.\ show that many
users hold incomplete or inaccurate models of how the internet handles
their data generally, with direct consequences for the privacy decisions
they make~\cite{kang2015mydata}. Ramokapane et al.\ study cloud-storage
deletion specifically and find users often cannot tell whether a delete
action removed data locally, from the cloud, or from other synced
devices, and adopt ad hoc coping strategies (e.g., deleting the same
item multiple times) in response to that uncertainty~\cite{ramokapane2017stupid}.
Habib et al.\ audit the deletion and opt-out controls of 150 websites
directly and find that even when a control exists, it is often
inconsistently located, under-explained, or effectively non-functional --
an interface-level failure mode distinct from, but compounding, any
backend failure to actually delete data~\cite{habib2019empirical}.

\textbf{LLM memory specifically.} On the user side specifically, Zhang
et al.\ interview 18 users of LLM chatbots with retrieval-augmented-
generation-based ``memory'' features and find diverse, often incomplete
mental models of what the memory stores and how it is used, alongside an
explicit desire for finer-grained control over reviewing, editing, and
deleting individual memories~\cite{zhang2025understanding}.

\textbf{Positioning.} Li et al.\ show that LLM platforms' stated
deletion controls are inconsistent by design~\cite{li2026privacy}; no
identified prior work tests whether those (or comparable) controls
work as stated on live accounts, or pairs such a test with a matched
participant survey of what the same controls are believed and used to
accomplish. This paper reports both halves of that pairing: a technical
audit of whether six commercial platforms' deletion controls remove the
data they claim to (RQ1, RQ2), and a participatory survey of which
deletion methods people actually choose, how those choices and their
perceived cost/protection change with data sensitivity, and where
expectation and verification behavior part ways with what the methods
are actually likely to do (RQ3, RQ4;
\S\ref{sec:sample-knowledge}), which allows the two to be compared directly
against each other (RQ5).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Participatory Survey}
\label{sec:survey-methodology}

The survey uses a within-subject design: the same participants rate two
scenario blocks in fixed order, a less-sensitive and a more-sensitive
data-deletion vignette, each repeating an identical question battery so
scenario is the only manipulated factor. Each scenario battery includes
three multi-item Likert scales (perceived protection with 5 items, effort
with 4 items, and benefit loss with 3 items) plus single-item measures of
deletion method choice, willingness to delete, deletion expectations, and
verification behavior. All six scale-by-scenario combinations meet
conventional reliability thresholds (Cronbach's $\alpha=0.87$--$0.92$),
confirmed identically by both analysis pipelines described in
\S\ref{sec:sample-knowledge}.

\subsection{Deletion-Request Prompt Corpus}
\label{sec:prompt-corpus}

One arm of the technical audit (\S\ref{sec:audit-methodology}) types real
deletion requests into each platform rather than researcher-authored
phrasings. Those requests originate in the same instrument described in
\S\ref{sec:survey-methodology}: after selecting a deletion method within
each scenario block, participants typed the exact message they would send
the assistant to carry it out and stated their rationale for that
phrasing. Across both scenario blocks this yielded 415 raw responses from
209 participants; after removing blank/unusable entries and within- and
cross-participant duplicates, 375 distinct prompts remained. Each was
scrubbed of residual personally identifying text (32 of 375 prompts
altered: 20 organization-name, 12 location-name, and 3 person-name
redactions) and passed through an
automated feature-extraction pass labeling candidate dimensions including
a coarse deletion-location category, used only to stratify the sampling
step below, not as the reliability-bearing codes reported next.

\textbf{Coding and reliability.} Two coders hand-coded prompts against a
seven-dimension codebook: deletion location (the storage surface the
request names: the current conversation, the assistant's persistent
memory, the account as a whole, a named backend or database, or a
forward-looking ``stop using this'' instruction that names no location),
justification, data type (\emph{what}), grammatical mood, tone, verb
register, and any accompanying secondary request (for example, asking for
confirmation, or asking the assistant to preserve everything else the
deletion target excludes). Each dimension was coded on 50 items.
Inter-rater reliability was computed with Gwet's $AC_1$~\cite{gwet2008ac1}
rather than Cohen's $\kappa$: several dimensions have highly skewed
category distributions (one label covering 88--96\% of items), under which
$\kappa$'s chance-agreement term is known to understate true
agreement~\cite{gwet2008ac1,wongpakaran2013comparison}.
Table~\ref{tab:ac1} reports the final values. All seven dimensions reached
$AC_1\geq0.807$, and pooled across all 350 ratings, agreement was 90.9\%
($AC_1=0.905$).

Deletion location initially agreed least, so the coders met to resolve
its disagreements and tightened its definition. A location counts only
when the prompt names it with an explicit referent (conversation or chat,
memory, account, or a database, server, or training data); quantifiers
such as ``all'' and generic containers such as ``history'' or
``messages'' do not qualify. When a prompt names several locations, a
named database governs over a conversation or memory, and a
forward-looking clause governs over a named conversation. Deletion
location was then coded on a fresh set of 50 items under the revised
definition, and the value in Table~\ref{tab:ac1} comes from that set,
after the coders compared their codes and revised four of them. The other
six dimensions were coded once, on the original 50 items.

\textbf{Sampling for the technical audit.} A 138-item sample was drawn
from the 375-item corpus by proportional stratified sampling on the
automated first-pass deletion-location label (a floor was applied so that
low-frequency categories were not eliminated from the draw), for use
verbatim as the chat-typed erasure request in the technical audit's
NL-forget-prompt cells (\S\ref{sec:audit-methodology}). This sample was
fixed as a specific item list once distributed to both coders for the
reliability pass above, rather than regenerable by rerunning the sampling
step under a different random seed, since redrawing would have
desynchronized the sampled items from the completed coding.

\subsection{Technical Audit}
\label{sec:audit-methodology}

The technical audit is a black-box behavioral test of six commercial LLM
chatbot platforms' consumer-facing deletion controls (Claude, ChatGPT,
Gemini, Copilot, Perplexity, and DeepSeek), conducted against each
platform's live web interface rather than its API, since the API does
not expose the memory, settings, and deletion surfaces the study's
research questions concern. Each platform is tested through a
researcher-controlled account under browser automation
(Playwright)\footnote{Two of the six platforms additionally require a
stealth plugin to pass Cloudflare bot-detection checks that otherwise
block any automation-driven session; see the Ethics Considerations
section.}, driven by an orchestrator that loads each test cell's
parameters from a pre-registered experiment design and dispatches it to
the matching platform-specific implementation.

\textbf{Design.} A \textit{cell} pairs one injection method with one
erasure method on one platform. Three injection methods test distinct
memory-formation pathways: \textit{I1}, an offhand conversational mention
with no explicit request to remember it, testing whether a platform
infers memory-worthiness on its own; \textit{I2}, an explicit
``please remember'' instruction in conversation; and \textit{I3}, a
direct entry into a platform's memory/settings field where one exists. A
fourth condition, \textit{FILE}, tests document-based disclosure by
uploading a PDF containing the same disclosure content instead of typing
it. Erasure methods are drawn from each platform's own available
controls and range from conversation-level deletion, through per-item and
bulk memory deletion, to account- or privacy-dashboard-level erasure,
reflecting that no two platforms expose an identical set of deletion
surfaces. The full design comprises 88 cells: 76 platform/injection/
erasure combinations plus 12 file-upload cells, distributed unevenly
across platforms in proportion to how many memory and deletion surfaces
each one actually exposes.

\textbf{NL-forget-prompt extension.} The 138-item prompt sample described
in \S\ref{sec:prompt-corpus} is run as an additional, additive set of 828
cells (138 prompts $\times$ 6 platforms), appended to rather than
replacing the 88-cell design above. Each prompt is typed verbatim, with
only its disclosed referent substituted in, as the erasure request against
every platform, using the fixed I1 (offhand conversational) injection
method for every cell in this extension: the erasure action here is
already a typed chat message, so injection type is held constant to
isolate erasure-phrasing coverage as the varying factor. This extension
tests a broader, participant-authored sample of real erasure phrasing
against the same three-probe recall design (R1/R2/R3) as the 88-cell
battery, rather than the small number of researcher-authored
representative phrasings a fixed-prompt design would require.

\textbf{Contamination control.} Accounts are reused across cells rather
than provisioned fresh per cell; attribution across cells sharing an
account is instead secured by giving every cell a unique \textit{referent}
(the conversational topic disclosed, e.g. a hobby or habit) and a unique
\textit{token} (a distinctive codename attached to that referent, 2--4
words, drawn to give each token a quantifiable, individually low guess
probability, following the canary-design rationale of prior work on
measuring unintended memorization~\cite{carlini2019secret}). A recall
probe that surfaces a cell's token is attributable to that cell alone.
Disclosure content is restricted by a narrow exclusion list (weapons,
drugs, sexual content, profanity, and a slur/explicit-term denylist)
chosen to avoid triggering platform content filters without otherwise
constraining topic naturalism.

Token uniqueness alone is not sufficient where a cell's own erasure
action is not scoped to that cell's referent at all: an account-wide or
``clear everything'' action reaches every other cell sharing that
account regardless of referent. Two further account-provisioning rules
address this. First, within the 88-cell battery, any cell whose erasure
action shares an account with a sibling cell testing the same
account-wide surface, or with a cell still inside its 31-day post-erasure
recall window, is moved to its own dedicated account before either cell
runs, so that a ``confirmed erased'' or ``not found'' recall outcome can
be attributed to the mechanism under test rather than an unrelated
blanket action that happened to share its account. Second, cross-platform
account reuse does not require this isolation: the same login credential
is deliberately reused across multiple platforms because each platform's
memory and conversation history is siloed to its own backend, confirmed
directly in this study: an account-wide erasure action executed on one
platform had no observed reach into a different platform sharing the
same login credential. For the NL-forget-prompt extension specifically,
each platform's 138 cells share a single dedicated account (distinct from
the 88-cell battery's accounts) rather than one account per cell, since
per-cell token uniqueness is sufficient for the subset of prompts whose
coded deletion location (\S\ref{sec:prompt-corpus}) names a narrower
surface than the account itself. The remaining subset, whose deletion
location codes as account-wide, is excluded from that shared account and
held pending provisioning of a dedicated account per item, since running
an account-wide request there would erase every other cell's
still-untested content sharing that account.

\textbf{Temporal design.} Each cell runs as three separately scheduled
events rather than in immediate succession: erasure occurs 48 hours
after injection, and recall occurs 31 days after erasure. This window is
sourced from the platforms' own stated data-deletion completion
commitments, not an arbitrary delay. Recall is tested two ways within
that single 31-day-post-erasure phase: \textit{same-session} (probing
within one continuous browser session) and \textit{cross-session} (a
separate session against the same account), both run back to back rather
than at separately staggered times.

\textbf{Recall probes.} Three probe types run at each recall point.
\textit{R1} (direct) first asks an open question about the disclosed
referent, then, only if the open question fails to surface the token,
follows up with a forced-choice question against three
length-matched distractor tokens, a design informed by prior
ranking-against-counterfactuals approaches to quantifying personal-data
memorization~\cite{staufer2025wikimem}. \textit{R2} (settings) directly
inspects the platform's own memory-listing interface where one exists.
\textit{R3} (indirect) sends an unrelated task that never mentions the
token, testing whether the disclosed content leaks into unrelated
generation rather than only being recoverable when directly asked about.
R1 and R3 responses are auto-scored by case-insensitive token match, with
a validated correction: a forced-choice reply counts as a genuine
recovery only if it names the correct token while mentioning at most one
of the three distractors, since a hedge or refusal that restates the
full option list otherwise produces a false-positive match.

\textbf{Outcome recording and repetition.} Every cell's erasure and
recall outcome is pre-registered against a fixed taxonomy (complied /
partial / refused / deflected / silently ignored for erasure; token
found / not found / ambiguous, auto-scored with a human-review flag, for
recall) before full-battery runs begin. Each cell runs once; a cell is
rerun only if its observed outcome deviates from its pre-registered
expected outcome, before that deviation is recorded as a finding. Every
phase of every cell (injection, erasure, same-session recall,
cross-session recall) saves a full transcript and a full-page screenshot,
retained per platform and cell for independent review. All six accounts
are provisioned on each platform's free, no-cost tier; where a platform
gates a tested surface behind a paid tier, that is recorded as a result
rather than treated as a methodology gap. File-upload cells (\textit{FILE})
use a distinct, more granular verification taxonomy reflecting that a
document can be read into a conversation, into persistent memory, into
both, or into neither without a follow-up request. Extraction is
checked passively first (does the platform's own unprompted reply or
memory listing surface the token) and, only if neither does, once more
after an explicit follow-up request to summarize the document.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{What Users Know About Memory and Deletion}
\label{sec:sample-knowledge}

Of 219 responses, 37 were excluded: 1 for failing the attention check and 36 for having never deleted chatbot data. This left 182 eligible participants, including $n=177$ who completed both sensitivity scenarios.

\textbf{Participants were young, educated, and experienced chatbot users.}
Seventy-three percent were under 45, 58.8\% identified as male, and 62.1\% held at least a bachelor's degree (Table~\ref{tab:demographics}). All were US-based with prior chatbot experience. ChatGPT (99.4\%) and Gemini (92.7\%) were nearly universal, while Claude and Copilot were each used by 67.8\%. Perplexity and DeepSeek were the least used and most recently adopted platforms (Figure~\ref{fig:ai-tenure}).

\textbf{Privacy knowledge was high, with one notable gap.}
Participants averaged 5.06/6 correct on questions covering the right to be forgotten, chatbot memory, and training data; 46.3\% answered all six correctly. Accuracy was lowest for whether chatbots access only public user data (70.1\%) and highest for whether conversations may be used for future model training (93.8\%).

\subsection{Deletion Expectations and the Sensitivity Effect}
\label{sec:sensitivity-effect}

\textbf{Deleting a single conversation was the dominant choice regardless of sensitivity.}
It was selected by 84/177 (47.5\%) participants in the less-sensitive scenario and 80/177 (45.2\%) in the more-sensitive scenario, compared with 33 and 40 selecting ``Clear all conversation history,'' respectively (Figure~\ref{fig:method-selection}). Only four methods met the $n\geq14$ threshold for inferential testing across both scenarios; the remaining three are reported descriptively. Overall method selection did not differ significantly by sensitivity (Stuart-Maxwell, $p=.393$).

\centering

\textbf{Methods differ in effort, but not in protection or benefit loss.} ``Delete a specific saved memory or fact'' had the highest pooled effort ($M=2.45$) and benefit-loss ($M=3.43$) ratings, whereas ``Delete this single conversation'' had the lowest pooled effort ($M=1.68$). Protection ratings varied little across methods (pooled range: 3.65--3.80; Figure~\ref{fig:crowning-combined}).

\textbf{No method was statistically superior across any outcome.} Kruskal-Wallis tests found no differences in protection or benefit loss in the pooled, less-sensitive, or more-sensitive analyses (all $p>.15$). Effort differed significantly across methods in all three scopes (pooled $H=19.54$, $p<.001$; less-sensitive $H=18.42$, $p<.001$; more-sensitive $H=16.98$, $p<.001$), but no method outperformed all others in pairwise comparisons. ``Delete this single conversation'' required significantly less effort than ``Delete a specific saved memory or fact'' in the pooled and less-sensitive analyses ($p<.001$), but did not differ from ``Clear all conversation history'' or asking the chatbot to forget the information. In the more-sensitive scenario, asking the chatbot to forget had the lowest mean effort, but no pairwise differences were significant. Thus, the data support only that deleting a single conversation is lower-effort than deleting a specific saved memory, not that it is universally the lowest-effort method.

\textbf{Greater data sensitivity increases willingness to delete and perceived protection, but not effort or benefit loss.} Among the same $n=177$ participants, willingness to delete increased from $M=3.98$ to $M=4.37$ ($\Delta=+0.39$, $p<.001$, $r=.55$), the largest effect observed, while perceived protection increased from $M=3.66$ to $M=3.79$ ($\Delta=+0.13$, $p<.001$, $r=.38$). Effort ($\Delta=+0.03$) and benefit loss ($\Delta=+0.04$) did not change significantly. Thus, although protection ratings do not differ across deletion methods, they increase when the data is more sensitive. 

\textbf{More than one-third of participants switch deletion methods as sensitivity increases.} While 64\% (113/176) selected the same method across scenarios, 36\% (63/176) switched. The overall distribution remained unchanged (Stuart-Maxwell, stat$=6.28$, $df=6$, $p=.393$). ``Clear all conversation history'' gained the most users ($+7$), whereas asking the chatbot to forget the information lost the most ($-11$; 15 dropped vs.\ 4 added, $p=.019$). No other method changed significantly, suggesting that greater sensitivity specifically reduces reliance on conversational deletion requests.

\textbf{Participants expect deletion to remove data, but rarely expect it to prevent training use or know how to verify deletion.} Deletion expectations and verification behaviors differed significantly within each scenario (less-sensitive: expectation $Q=245.25$, $p<.001$; verification $Q=142.99$, $p<.001$; both patterns replicated in the more-sensitive scenario). ``Permanently deleted'' was the most common expectation (55.4\% less-sensitive; 62.4\% more-sensitive), whereas only 15--37\% expected deletion to prevent future training use. Approximately 45\% did not know how to verify deletion, making this the most common verification response in both scenarios (Figure~\ref{fig:expectation-verification}).

\textbf{Verification behavior varies by deletion method, while expectations
largely do not.}
In the less-sensitive scenario, method choice was associated with asking in
the same conversation ($p<.001$), asking in a new conversation ($p=.002$),
and not knowing how to verify deletion ($p=.001$). In the more-sensitive
scenario, significant differences appeared for asking in the same
conversation ($p=.005$), checking settings ($p=.006$), and not knowing how
to verify ($p=.044$). Expectations were comparatively stable: none differed
by method in the less-sensitive scenario, while two did in the
more-sensitive scenario: whether deleted information would be referenced
in the current ($p=.008$) or future conversation ($p=.030$). Together with
the effort results (Figure~\ref{fig:crowning-combined}), these findings
suggest that lower-effort deletion methods are not necessarily easier for
users to verify.

\textbf{Verification increases with confidence, but remains uncommon.}
Higher confidence was associated with greater verification in both scenarios
(less-sensitive: $z=2.50$, $p=.012$; more-sensitive: $z=2.23$, $p=.026$).
Yet among participants ``not at all confident,'' 81.8\% and 88.9\%,
respectively, never verified deletion, showing no improvement with greater
sensitivity.

\textbf{Ideal deletion methods differ substantially from those actually used.}
Used-versus-ideal choices differed for nearly every method in the
less-sensitive scenario (all $p<.001$ except ``Delete my account'') and all
seven methods in the more-sensitive scenario ($p\leq.022$). Participants
consistently selected more methods as ideal than they actually used, with
the gap widening for ``Ask chatbot to forget'' (32.8 to 42.1 percentage
points) and ``Delete a specific saved memory'' (20.3 to 29.2).

\textbf{Age and chatbot tenure affect method choice, but not deletion ratings.}
Neither age nor ChatGPT tenure was associated with protection, effort,
benefit loss, or willingness to delete (all $p>.14$). Age predicted method
choice only in the less-sensitive scenario ($p=.015$, $V=.229$), while
ChatGPT tenure did so only in the more-sensitive scenario ($p=.013$,
$V=.207$).

\textbf{Protection, effort, and benefit loss capture distinct constructs.}
Pairwise correlations were below the $|\rho|\geq.8$ redundancy threshold
(Table~\ref{tab:discriminant-validity}). Protection was unrelated to effort
($\rho=-.057$) and benefit loss ($\rho=.129$), while effort and benefit loss
were modestly correlated ($\rho=.305$, $p<.001$).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The training-data blind spot mirrors a real technical
distinction.} Deletion that removes a record does not, by itself, remove
that record's influence on a model already trained on it; this is the
gap machine-unlearning research treats as a distinct, harder technical
problem from record-level deletion~\cite{bourtoule2021machine}.
Participants collapse that distinction: most expect a deletion action to
leave data ``permanently deleted,'' while 65--85\% do not expect it to
stop that data from being used in future model training
(\S\ref{sec:sample-knowledge}). Whether that expectation gap reflects an
accurate reading of what today's chatbot deletion controls actually
guarantee, or a mismatch between user belief and platform behavior, is a
question this paper's technical audit (RQ2/RQ5) takes up directly; either way, the
policy language on which participants would have to rely to form this
belief does not appear to resolve the distinction for them.

\textbf{Low verification is a recurring failure mode, not a new one.}
Usable-privacy work on other deletion surfaces has repeatedly found that
a deletion control's existence does not guarantee it is understood,
locatable, or actually checked. This holds for cloud-storage
deletion~\cite{ramokapane2017stupid} and for website data-deletion and
opt-out controls audited directly~\cite{habib2019empirical}.
Participants' verification behavior fits the same pattern in a new
setting: roughly 45\% did not know how to check whether a chatbot
deletion worked, and confidence in a method did not translate into
higher sensitivity-scenario verification rates
(\S\ref{sec:sample-knowledge}). This suggests the verification gap is a
property of how deletion controls are generally designed and explained,
not something specific to any one platform's implementation.

\textbf{The memory-deletion cost matches a known mental-model gap.}
Interview work on LLM chatbot memory features finds that users hold
diverse, often incomplete models of what a stored ``memory'' represents
and how it was formed, and want finer-grained control over
it~\cite{zhang2025understanding}. That gives a plausible mechanism for
this paper's own finding that ``delete a specific saved memory'' is both
the highest-effort and highest-benefit-loss method participants
rated (\S\ref{sec:sample-knowledge}): if a user is unsure what a memory
entry represents or how it was derived, deciding whether and how to
delete it is harder than deleting a single conversation turn, independent
of how many clicks either action actually takes.

\textbf{Demand for heavier controls rises with sensitivity faster than
platforms (or users' own behavior) keep up.} Willingness to delete and
perceived protection both increase significantly with data sensitivity,
but the gap between participants' used and ideal deletion methods widens
rather than narrows in the more-sensitive scenario
(\S\ref{sec:sample-knowledge}). Li et al.'s walkthrough finds that
LLM platforms' deletion controls are already inconsistently implemented
across accounts and message types~\cite{li2026privacy}; taken together,
these results suggest that exactly when users want more control, the
controls available to exercise, and the ones users actually reach for,
both lag behind that demand, for reasons this paper's user-side data
alone cannot fully separate (control availability vs. control
discoverability vs. user effort tolerance).

\textbf{Implication.} Across all four points above, the same shape
recurs: participants' beliefs about deletion are neither random nor
uniformly wrong, but systematically optimistic about scope (assuming
permanence and training exclusion) and systematically under-informed
about verification. A chatbot platform that wants its deletion controls
to function as a real right-to-erasure mechanism, rather than a
lightweight convenience action, would need to close both gaps
concurrently: stating plainly what a given control does and does not
remove, and making it possible to confirm the outcome. Closing
only one leaves the other to keep producing the mismatch RQ5 is designed
to measure directly.
