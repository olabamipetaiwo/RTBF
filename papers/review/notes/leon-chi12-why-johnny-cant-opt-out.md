# Leon, Ur, Balebako, Cranor, Shay, Wang. Why Johnny Can't Opt Out: A Usability Evaluation of Tools to Limit Online Behavioral Advertising

ACM CHI 2012 (May 5-10, 2012, Austin), pp. 589-598 (first page 589 and last page 598 printed in the PDF; DOI 10.1145/2207676.2207759 as in the manifest). File: `4_users/leon-chi12-why-johnny-cant-opt-out.pdf` (ACM PDF, downloaded by the user through the UCF library). Read the abstract, introduction, the tools and method in outline, the limitations, the discussion and the conclusion. I skimmed the per-tool results.

## Question
Can users install, configure and use the tools that limit online behavioral advertising (OBA), and do the tools do what users think they do (Abstract)?

## Method
- Laboratory study with 45 participants recruited from the Pittsburgh region (Craigslist, flyers), paid $30; five participants for each of nine tools: browser plugins that block requests to URLs, tools that set opt-out cookies, and browsers' built-in privacy settings. Participants were interviewed about OBA, then configured and used a tool for about an hour while their behavior and attitudes were recorded (Section 3).

## Findings
- "Serious usability flaws in all nine tools" examined (Conclusion). Many tools were difficult to configure and their default settings were often minimally protective (Abstract).
- Ineffective communication, confusing interfaces and no feedback led many participants to conclude that a tool was blocking OBA when it had not been properly configured to do so (Abstract). This is the point we use: a control can look as if it works without working.
- Opt-out cookies are undone when users delete their cookies, unless a tool such as TACO is used (Section 4): an example of one control silently reversing another.
- The authors conclude that "the current approach to OBA self-regulation through opt-out tools is fundamentally flawed".

## Limits the authors state
Participants from one region, not representative; no statistical conclusions drawn; a lab study, with about one hour on each tool (Limitations).

## How it differs from ours
- Opt-out of tracking, not deletion of data. It measures whether users can set up and believe the tool, and it shows users mistaking a non-working control for a working one. It does not check what the tool did on the server side. This is a 2012 example of the same user-side-only evaluation we are going beyond.

## Use in our paper
- Cite once, early in the privacy-controls paragraph, for "users can believe a control is working when it is not, because the interface gives no feedback". Peripheral to deletion; first to cut if the list needs trimming.
