# Bumiller, Delgado Rodriguez, Mecke, Distler, Alt. Too Many Zombies: Exploring Challenges and Motivations for (Not) Deleting Unused Online Accounts

ACM CHI 2026 (April 13-17, 2026, Barcelona), 20 pages, DOI 10.1145/3772318.3790497 (from the PDF's reference block). File: `4_users/bumiller-chi26-too-many-zombies.pdf`. Read the abstract, introduction, the method for both studies, the results on zombie-account counts and deletion challenges, and the limitations. I skimmed the design recommendations and the trigger/moment ratings.

## Question
How many unused ("zombie") accounts do users hold, why do they not delete them, and when and how could a tool prompt them to (Abstract)?

## Method
- Accounts study: online survey of 120 US participants on Prolific, each reviewing one of 12 non-overlapping subsets (about 216 services each) of a list assembled from JustDeleteMe and Tranco, marking services they knew, had an account with, and no longer used (Section 5).
- Challenges study: second online survey of 100 US participants on Prolific on awareness of zombie accounts, motivations, suitable moments, triggers and support wishes, using an 86-service list drawn from the first study (Section 6).

## Findings
- 110 of 120 participants reported at least one zombie account. Extrapolating from group means, a typical user would know about 341 services by name, have had an account on 134 and hold about 53 zombie accounts, nearly 40% of all created accounts (Section 5.6.2). This is an extrapolation from subsets, not a direct count.
- In the challenges study participants marked on average 5.63 of 86 listed accounts as zombies; seven marked none, and 20 marked 10 or more (Section 6.6.2).
- Participants reported high self-efficacy but underestimated how many zombie accounts they had (Abstract).
- Among 86 participants who had tried to delete an account, most had succeeded, but over half had failed at least once or hit challenges: complicated procedures (n = 36), hidden or hard-to-find deletion settings (n = 25), customer-support contact needed (n = 17), extensive verification (n = 10), lost credentials (n = 8), and providers offering only deactivation (n = 4).
- Promising moments to prompt deletion: when updating account information or setting up a new device; triggers that raised willingness: breach notifications and data sensitivity.

## Limits the authors state
Self-reported data measure intentions, not behavior, and carry self-report and social-desirability bias; US-only sample, so applicability elsewhere needs study (Limitations).

## How it differs from ours
- It concerns the user's decision and difficulty in initiating deletion of accounts. It does not check what the services do after the user asks. The reported "deactivation instead of deletion" is a user-reported instance of the logical-versus-persistent distinction in group 1.

## Use in our paper
- Cite for the user-side barriers to deletion (hard-to-find settings, complicated procedures, deactivation offered instead of deletion) with the counts above, and for the point that users hold many accounts they no longer use, which leave data behind. Keep "53 zombie accounts" labelled as an extrapolation.
