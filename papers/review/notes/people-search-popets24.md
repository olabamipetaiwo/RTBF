# Take, Gallagher, Young, Forte, Bhalerao, McCoy, Greenstadt. What to Expect When You're Accessing: An Exploration of User Privacy Rights in People Search Websites

PoPETs 2024(4), pp. 311-326, DOI 10.56553/popets-2024-0118 (author list, pages and issue read from the PDF itself; this closes the open check on them). File: `2_audits/people-search-popets24.pdf` (open-access PoPETs copy). ## Question
How usable and effective are the data access and removal mechanisms of 20 people-search websites (a kind of data broker), and are the sites connected, so that removal from one affects others (Abstract, Section 1)?

## Method
- Researchers in different places (GDPR and CCPA settings) used their own real data and recorded their own experiences of access requests, paid reports and removal requests (Section 1, Section 3). Researcher observation was chosen on purpose, instead of a user study.
- Two-step removal (Section 5): first request removal from four suspected parent sites (Intelius, BeenVerified, PeopleFinders, Whitepages), wait two weeks, check the remaining sites for effects, then request removal from the sites that still showed the data. Ten sites received at least one removal request in total.
- A second self-search two months after the first removal round tested for reappearance.

## Findings
- Data access requests were mostly unsuccessful: sites cited legal exceptions (for example the CCPA's "public information" carve-out) or misread the request; only one group (BeenVerified) returned the same report that it sells (Abstract).
- Removal was "more streamlined than data access, but not very transparent": questions about the scope of removal and reappearance remain (Abstract). Information about removal scope, duration and reappearance was incomplete on the sites; six addressed reappearance and few took measures against it (Section 5).
- After removals from four fee sites, ten other sites had removed the information (Figure 3); the researchers inferred connected ownership: 14 of the 20 sites belong to four groups.
- Verification difficulty: on two sites a paywall hid all results, so only two researchers with uncommon names could see "0 results"; for the rest, success could not be checked without paying (Section 5).
- No reappearance was observed two months later on sites where removal had succeeded; BeenVerified answered a later access request by citing the removal date and saying it no longer held the data (Section 5).

## Limits the authors state
Researchers are more educated and privacy-aware than typical users, so the real burden is probably higher; the aim was to study the sites, not user behavior; findings come from a small number of researchers' own records (Limitations).

## How it differs from ours
- It audits a different class of services (data brokers) with the researchers' own identities. It is the closest peer-reviewed example of a "remove, then check whether it reappears after weeks" design.
- Its core difficulty matches ours: success can be checked only through what the service lets you see, and the check can itself be blocked (paywall). Their result "removal in one place removed it in connected places" is the opposite of the cross-chat and sibling-token issues in our design, where the question is whether a deletion spreads or fails to.

## Use in our paper
- Cite for an audit of removal mechanisms on data brokers, for the two-month reappearance check, and for the observation that scope, duration and reappearance are rarely stated.
- Note: it is a researcher-experience study of 20 sites, so state sample size when quoting.
